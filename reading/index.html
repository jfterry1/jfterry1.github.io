November 2026

  1. Praying for Rain

    José-Antonio Espín-Sánchez, Salvador Gil-Guirado, Nicholas Ryan · Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2026

    A religious leader can only win believers by praying for rain where the chance of rain rises the longer a dry spell lasts, so prayers land when rain is most likely anyway. In Murcia, Spain, the church's prayers predict rainfall over two centuries, and ethnic groups facing that kind of rainfall pattern are 47% more likely to pray for rain.

October 2026

  1. Artificial Intelligence and the Labor Market

    Menaka Hampole, Dimitris Papanikolaou, Lawrence D.W. Schmidt, Bryan Seegmiller · NBER Working Paper 33509, 2025

    Builds task-level measures of AI exposure from 2010 to 2023. Labor demand falls when an occupation's tasks are exposed on average, but rises when the exposure is concentrated in a few tasks, since workers can shift effort elsewhere. Net employment effects are modest because AI-adopting firms grow.

    laborai & technology

  2. Trading Goods for Lives: NAFTA's Mortality Impacts and Implications

    Amy Finkelstein, Matthew J. Notowidigdo, Steven X. Shi · NBER Working Paper 34855, 2026

    Areas more exposed to Mexican import competition after NAFTA saw mortality rise for the next 15 years, especially among working-age men. The broader lesson: job losses in manufacturing raise local mortality, while non-manufacturing contractions lower it, which helps reconcile the trade-shock and recession literatures.

    healthtradelabor

August 2026

  1. Recessions, Mortality, and Migration Bias: Evidence from the Lancashire Cotton Famine

    Vellore Arthi, Brian Beach, W. Walker Hanlon · American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 14(2), 2022

    Uses the cotton shortage from the U.S. Civil War as a downturn in Lancashire and shows that out-migration from hard-hit areas biases standard estimates. After correcting for it, the recession raised mortality among cotton households, with spillovers to local service workers.

    healthbusiness cycleseconomic history

June 2026

  1. Labor Market Strength and Declining Community College Enrollment

    Joshua Goodman, Joseph Winkelmann · NBER Working Paper 34498, 2025

    The post-2010 drop in U.S. college enrollment is a community college story, and about a third of it is just colleges being reclassified as four-year. A 1-point rise in local unemployment raises first-time community college enrollment by about 2%, so stronger labor markets explain roughly 60% of the post-Great Recession decline.

    educationlaborbusiness cycles

January 2026

  1. Short- and Long-Term Effects of Unemployment on Fertility

    Janet Currie, Hannes Schwandt · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2014

    Follows cohorts of U.S.-born women across 140 million birth records. Unemployment in the early 20s cuts fertility in the short run, and the loss grows by age 40 rather than being made up later, driven largely by women who remain childless.

    fertilitybusiness cycles

November 2025

  1. Are Recessions Good for Your Health?

    Christopher J. Ruhm · Quarterly Journal of Economics 115(2), 2000

    The classic state-panel result that mortality is procyclical: total deaths and most causes of death fall when unemployment rises, with suicide the main exception. Healthier behavior when the economy weakens (less smoking and obesity, more exercise) is part of the story.

    healthbusiness cycles

October 2025

  1. Lives vs. Livelihoods: The Impact of the Great Recession on Mortality and Welfare

    Amy Finkelstein, Matthew J. Notowidigdo, Frank Schilbach, Jonathan Zhang · NBER Working Paper 32110, 2024

    Commuting zones hit harder by the Great Recession saw age-adjusted mortality fall by about 2.3%, an effect that lasted at least a decade and was concentrated among less-educated adults. Much of it runs through lower air pollution, which meaningfully lowers the welfare cost of recessions.

    healthbusiness cycles

September 2025

  1. Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change: Evidence from U.S. Résumé and Job Posting Data

    Seyed M. Hosseini, Guy Lichtinger · SSRN Working Paper, 2025

    Résumé data on tens of millions of workers, with firm GenAI adoption flagged by "GenAI integrator" job postings. After adoption, junior employment falls sharply relative to non-adopters while senior employment holds up.

    laborai & technology

February 2025

  1. Why Being Wrong Can Be Right: Magical Warfare Technologies and the Persistence of False Beliefs

    Nathan Nunn, Raul Sanchez de la Sierra · American Economic Review: Papers & Proceedings, 2017

    Bulletproofing spells in eastern Congo are false, but believing in them gets villagers to fight militias together. A belief that is wrong for the individual can be right for the group, which helps explain why it persists.

    culturedevelopment

  2. Corruption, Norms, and Legal Enforcement: Evidence from Diplomatic Parking Tickets

    Raymond Fisman, Edward Miguel · Journal of Political Economy 115(6), 2007

    UN diplomats in Manhattan had immunity from parking enforcement, so their unpaid tickets reveal norms alone: diplomats from high-corruption countries racked up far more. When New York gained the power to confiscate plates in 2002, violations dropped sharply, so enforcement matters too.

    culturepolitical economy

January 2025

  1. Superstition and Risk Taking: Evidence from "Zodiac Year" Beliefs in China

    Raymond Fisman, Wei Huang, Bo Ning, Yue Pan, Jiaping Qiu, Yongxiang Wang · Management Science 69(9), 2023

    Chinese tradition says your zodiac year (every 12 years from your birth year) brings bad luck. People favor safer investments in their zodiac year, and firms cut risk-taking, R&D, and acquisitions in their chairman's zodiac year.

    behavioralculture

November 2024

  1. The Clan and the Corporation: Sustaining Cooperation in China and Europe

    Avner Greif, Guido Tabellini · Journal of Comparative Economics 45(1), 2017

    Over the last millennium, cooperation in China was organized around kin-based clans and in Europe around self-governing corporations like cities. Morality and institutions reinforced each other, sending the two regions down different paths.

    economic historyculture

  2. The Potato's Contribution to Population and Urbanization: Evidence from a Historical Experiment

    Nathan Nunn, Nancy Qian · Quarterly Journal of Economics 126(2), 2011

    Uses regional suitability for potatoes and the timing of their arrival from the Americas. The potato accounts for roughly a quarter of Old World population and urbanization growth between 1700 and 1900.

    economic historydevelopment

  3. The Transmission of Women's Fertility, Human Capital, and Work Orientation across Immigrant Generations

    Francine D. Blau, Lawrence M. Kahn, Albert Yung-Hsu Liu, Kerry L. Papps · Journal of Population Economics 26(2), 2013

    Second-generation women's fertility, schooling, and labor supply track those of their parents' immigrant generation, and fertility is passed down more strongly than labor supply or education.

    fertilityculturelabor

  1. On the Origins of Gender Roles: Women and the Plough

    Alberto Alesina, Paola Giuliano, Nathan Nunn · Quarterly Journal of Economics 128(2), 2013

    Societies that historically farmed with the plough, which favored male upper-body strength, have less equal gender norms and lower female labor force participation today. The pattern shows up even among second-generation immigrants in the U.S.

    cultureeconomic historylabor

  1. The Consequences of Radical Reform: The French Revolution

    Daron Acemoglu, Davide Cantoni, Simon Johnson, James A. Robinson · American Economic Review 101(7), 2011

    Parts of Germany occupied by the French got sweeping institutional reforms that broke the power of guilds and old elites. Those areas urbanized faster afterward, especially after 1850.

  1. Time Travel on Professional Profiles

    Nicholas Bloom, Gideon Moore, Lisa K. Simon, Caelan Wilkie-Rogers · NBER Working Paper 35546, 2026

    Monthly snapshots of Revelio Labs profile data show that about one in five established U.S. LinkedIn users go back and rewrite jobs they have already left, often around a job switch. That makes profile-based skill measures less of a historical record than they look, but the edits also reveal what workers think employers want: AI language surges after 2022 while remote-work and DEI language fade.
